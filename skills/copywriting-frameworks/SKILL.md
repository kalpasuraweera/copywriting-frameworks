# Copywriting Frameworks

A structured system for writing persuasive copy, distilled from a long-form course on direct-response copywriting. The core idea: **copy is downstream of thinking**. Don't start typing sentences — start by answering a fixed set of questions about the reader and the offer. Once those are answered, the words come quickly.

Work through this skill in order when writing something from scratch:
1. **Think** — the Six P's (who, why us, what they get, why trust, why now, how it works)
2. **Write** — the Five C's (the prose style)
3. **Structure** — hook, body, CTA
4. Only if relevant: offer/pricing strategy and client-acquisition tactics

If the user just wants a quick edit or a single hook, you don't need to run the full process out loud — but still silently apply it (know the avatar, know the promise) before writing.

## Step 1: Think — The Six P's of Copy Thinking

Before writing a single line, answer these six questions. If the user hasn't given you the answers, ask only the ones you can't reasonably infer or assume from context — don't interrogate them for all six if the brief already implies most of the answers.

| P | Question | Notes |
|---|----------|-------|
| **People** | Who *specifically* is this for? | Not "small business owners" — the beginner marathoner vs. the busy professional vs. the runner over 40 have different fears and need different promises, even for the same product. Naming the avatar precisely is what makes everything downstream sharper. |
| **Positioning** | Why choose this over every alternative? | Positioning is how the product/offer sits in the reader's head relative to competitors. The strongest positioning is usually a true, specific fact competitors have but never bothered to say out loud — not a made-up differentiator. |
| **Promise** | What do they *get* — as an outcome, not a feature? | Outcome, not deliverable. Run the **"so what?" test**: state the feature, ask "so what?", answer, ask "so what?" again, repeat 3-4x until you hit the emotional core outcome (identity, status, relief). |
| **Proof** | Why should they believe it? | Three tiers, weakest to strongest: (1) authority by association ("as seen on," "Dr. X recommends"), (2) your own results, (3) a client's story that *matches the avatar's identity* (proof is relative — a case study of someone who looks/sounds like the reader lands harder than a "better" but less relatable one). |
| **Priority** | Why act *now* instead of later? | People move faster away from pain than toward pleasure — lead with pain/cost-of-inaction when you can do so honestly. Three levers: scarcity (limited spots), urgency (a real deadline), and cost of inaction (what happens if they stay on their current path). Don't fabricate scarcity/urgency that isn't real. |
| **Process** | How does it actually work? | A good process (a) explains why other/obvious solutions fail, (b) creates a believable path, (c) feels different from the generic version, (d) is easy to explain in one line. Template: "Everyone thinks [X]. It's wrong because [Y]. The real answer is [Z]." |

**Building the avatar (People):** if the user has customer interviews, sales-call transcripts, reviews, or testimonials, mine them directly — the exact phrases customers use to describe their situation are better hooks than anything invented from scratch. If not, build an empathy map: current daily reality → future fears (worst case) → current frustrations → future goals (best case). Don't start writing until you have more material than you need.

**Never invent proof.** If the brief doesn't include real results, client names, testimonials, credentials, or product specs, don't make them up to fill the Proof slot — fabricated numbers read as generic and break trust the moment they're checked. Write a clearly bracketed placeholder instead (e.g. `[client result: X → Y in Z weeks]`), keep the claim in the shape it should take, and tell the user what proof to go collect. The same goes for product details you're assuming (prices, durations, features): flag them so the user can swap in the real ones.

**Sanity-check the offer itself.** If the brief implies a promise that's unsafe, unrealistic, or likely to backfire (e.g. an outcome the product can't honestly deliver for the stated audience), say so in one or two lines and position the copy around an honest version — overpromising is a positioning problem, not a wording problem.

## Step 2: Write — The Five C's

This is the prose style, once you know what to say.

1. **Clear** — the reader must understand before they can be persuaded. Cut anything ambiguous rather than trying to add clarifying words. The **firefighter test**: if you had to shout the point to someone across a burning room in one breath, what would you say? Write that.
2. **Concise** — no wasted words. Write as if every word costs money. Before finalizing, ask "what can I cut without losing meaning?"
3. **Concrete** — make it visualizable. Prefer a specific, imaginable detail over an abstract claim ("took a roofing company from 14 leads/month to 70" beats "we help businesses grow"). For anything abstract or emotional, zoom into a specific sensory moment rather than naming the abstraction.
4. **Conversational** — match the reader's actual spoken register, not a "professional" register learned in school. Read it out loud; if it doesn't sound like a real person talking to this specific audience, rewrite it. Match slang/formality to the in-group you're addressing.
5. **Cadence** — vary sentence length so the writing has rhythm instead of droning. A string of same-length sentences reads flat no matter how good the content is; mix short, medium, and one longer sentence that builds momentum. Read the draft aloud, or squint at it and look at the visual shape of line lengths — a flat, unbroken block is a sign to fix cadence.

Underlying rule: copy should be a clear window onto the offer, not a showcase for the writer's vocabulary. If the reader notices the writing, it's in the way.

## Step 3: Structure — Hook, Body, CTA

**Hook (headline / opening line):** does most of the work — assume ~5x more people see the hook than read the body, so it deserves a disproportionate share of the effort. Score a hook (or generate hook candidates) against:

> **hook strength ≈ (benefit × relevance × credibility) ÷ perceived effort to get that benefit elsewhere**

- *Benefit*: what do they get from reading/clicking?
- *Relevance*: how many people in the target group does this actually apply to?
- *Credibility*: why should they believe you specifically can deliver/explain this?
- *Perceived effort*: how hard would it normally be to get this benefit without you? (higher = better hook, because you're offering a shortcut)

**Body — pick a shape based on intent:**
- *Educational content* → **What / Why / How**: state the point → say why the reader should care → show how to act on it.
- *Story-driven content* → **But / Therefore**: replace a flat "and then... and then..." timeline with reversals ("I did X, *but* Y happened. *Therefore* Z."). Tension comes from the but/therefore turns, not from more detail.

**CTA — one CTA, one avatar, one offer per piece.** Use **Who / What / When / How**: who this is for → what you want them to do → by when → and the exact mechanism (click this, reply with this, book here). A CTA that tries to serve two audiences or two asks at once weakens both.

## Step 4 (when relevant): Offer & Value Framing

If the request is about pricing, positioning a service, or writing a pitch rather than a single piece of copy:

- **Value has exactly three levers**: save the customer time, make them money, or reduce their risk. If an offer isn't converting, it's almost always one of: (1) too much friction/effort required of the buyer, (2) the money/benefit isn't shown clearly enough, or (3) the buyer doesn't yet believe you can deliver — map the fix to the actual cause rather than just "make it sound better."
- **A strong offer removes risk from the buyer**, e.g. framing a result as a guarantee or contingent on performance, rather than only describing what's delivered.
- **Client-acquisition pitch (the "TAG" approach)**, useful when the user is trying to land a client or a role by pitching unsolicited: **T**arget a specific person/company → **A**udit their current situation in real depth (their public work, their existing funnel/content/positioning, where the gaps are) → **G**ift them a concrete, presentation-ready plan or sample work *before* asking for anything, sized to how the target actually consumes information (e.g., a short visual deck beats a long memo for a busy exec). The pitch itself becomes the proof of competence.

## Applying this end-to-end

When asked to write a piece of copy, work through it in this order and briefly show your thinking before the draft (unless the user just wants the final copy):

1. State the Six P's in a few lines (ask the user to confirm/fill gaps only if genuinely unclear).
2. Draft 2-3 hook options scored against the benefit/relevance/credibility/effort formula.
3. Write the body in the What/Why/How or But/Therefore shape.
4. Close with one CTA in Who/What/When/How form.
5. Pass the full draft through the Five C's as a final edit — read it back and cut, don't just check the boxes.

When asked to *critique* existing copy, diagnose it against the same structure: is the avatar (People) actually specific? Is the promise an outcome or just a feature list? Is there real proof, and does it match the avatar? Is there a genuine reason to act now? Then check prose style against the Five C's. Point out the single biggest lever first rather than a flat list of every issue.
